Today's security challenges

Authentication tools share one flaw: no cryptographic link to the person.

Passwords prove nothing.

Phished, shared and never tied to a person.

MFA stops attacks, not impostors.

It cannot show who is actually using it.

A voice is easy to fake.

Impersonation needs no access at all.

Identity-verification biometrics.

Exposed to deepfakes, siloed, and stored as a honeypot.

Device-native biometrics.

They unlock the device, not the identity, and fall back to a PIN.

Passkeys.

They rely on device biometrics, so a PIN can stand in for the person.

Actions happen, nothing links them.

No dynamic link to what the user approves.

No proof, no accountability.

No binding proof of who authorized each transaction.

None of these methods can prove that the person authenticating is the same person who enrolled.

The security paradox

Traditional biometrics protect access with data that can never be changed.

Server-side biometric storage creates honeypots. Device-native biometrics authenticate the device, not the person: under NIST guidance a biometric is not an authenticator on its own, only an activation factor that unlocks the device's key.* The bank never learns whose face unlocked it, or whether a PIN did.

Privacy-preserving biometrics resolve the paradox: no complete template is ever created, stored or transmitted. Not on the device, not on the server, not anywhere in between.

* NIST SP 800-63B implementation resources. pages.nist.gov

The regulatory shift

The burden of proof has moved to the bank. A bank that cannot prove who approved a payment will pay for it.

EU and UK: proof

When a customer disputes a payment, the bank must prove it was authenticated, and use of a credential is not, on its own, proof of authorization.1

UK: reimbursement

Victims of authorised push payment fraud must be reimbursed since October 2024.2

EU: liability

Agreed payment reforms make banks refund impersonation fraud and cover losses where fraud controls fall short.3

Global: shared responsibility

Singapore assigns phishing losses to banks and telcos that miss their duties.4
